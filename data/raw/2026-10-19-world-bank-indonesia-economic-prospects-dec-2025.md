## Executive Summary (heading as extracted: "Exe cutive S um m ary")
### I. Economic Update
Indonesia’s economy continues to demonstrate
resilience amid heightened global uncertainty.
Real GDP growth held at 5.0 percent year-on-year
over the first three quarters of 2025, supported by
robust investment and a notable, albeit temporary,
contribution from net exports, which benefited from
front-loaded goods trade and rising global demand
for commodities, particularly palm oil, iron, steel,
and gold. Growth is relatively broad based, with
services contributing the most to economic activity
and agriculture substantially rebounding following
favorable climate conditions and government support
programs. Inflation is picking up on the back of rallying
food prices but remains within the central bank’s target
band. Meanwhile, financial buffers are adequate and
the fiscal framework compliant with the national fiscal
rules. Yet, with private consumption—traditionally the
main driver for growth—softening, the durability of
growth increasingly hinges on strengthening household
purchasing power, including through improved job
quality and policies that boosts investment returns and
productivity.

While macroeconomic stability remains, challenges
arise from the labor market with impact on
household welfare. Private consumption’s contribution
to growth moderated slightly to 2.7 percentage
points (pp) (53.3 percent of GDP growth), down from
2.8 pp in 2024 (54.8 percent of GDP growth). This is
consistent with labor market indicators pointing to
persistent challenges in job quality. Employment rose
by 1.3 percent between August 2024 and August
2025, but gains were concentrated in lower-paying
sectors such as low-value services and agriculture.
Real wages have trended down since 2018, and the
structure of employment shows signs of polarization,
with middle-skilled jobs shrinking relative to both
low- and high-skilled segments. Youth entrants are
disproportionately finding low-tier informal jobs.
This is consistent with survey evidence of a growing
disconnect between objective welfare gains and
subjective perceptions: despite continuing declines in
monetary poverty, a rising share of households’ report
feeling poor. This sense of economic insecurity is more

pronounced among the middle class, amid higher
income volatility and subdued real wage dynamics.
These patterns weigh on household consumption
and reinforce precautionary saving, even as headline
macroeconomic indicators remain solid.

Government policy has responded with a mix
of targeted support and prudence, though
effectiveness is constrained by weak revenue. The
fiscal deficit widened to 2.0 percent of GDP by October
2025, up from 1.4 percent of GDP same period last year,
amid weaker revenues linked to easing commodity
prices, accelerated tax refunds, and SOE dividends
retained by Danantara. Despite this constrained
environment, the government delivered quarterly
stimulus packages totaling 0.5 percent of GDP, aimed
at bolstering consumption and job creation. Additional
spending was possible due to a budget efficiency drive
and underspending in some of the priority programs.
Accommodative financing conditions helped contain
borrowing costs, but interest payment to revenue
remains high at 20.5 percent (year-to-October).
Looking ahead, the 2026 Budget Law targets a 2.7
percent deficit with ambitious revenue goals; achieving
these will require strong tax administration and policy
efforts in a less favorable commodity prices setting to
safeguard space for growth-enhancing spending.
Monetary policy is becoming more accommodative
to support private sector credit, but transmission
channels have been uneven. With inflation contained,
Bank Indonesia (BI) lowered the policy rate by a
cumulative 150 basis points since September 2024 to
4.75 percent, and complemented rate cuts with new
macroprudential incentives aiming to encourage faster
pass-through to lending rates. Nonetheless, market
lending and deposit rates have been slow to adjust,
pointing to friction in monetary transmission channels.
Despite adequate system liquidity, nominal credit
growth slowed to 7.4 percent year-on-year in October
2025, suggesting constraints arising from demand for
bankable projects and cautious risk appetite. Easing
has also coincided with capital outflows and Rupiah
pressures, requiring market interventions from BI to


<!-- page 11 (printed p. 2) -->

maintain stability. Strengthening credit uptake will
therefore depend on improved investment sentiment
and credit infrastructure.

The outlook remains resilient, with risks broadly
balanced. Growth is projected to hold at 5.0 percent in
2026, rising to 5.2 percent in 2027 on a gradual pickup
in investment anchored by state-led investment via
Danantara, monetary easing to stimulate private
sector credit, and Foreign Direct Investment (FDI).

Low inflation and fiscal stimulus will support private
consumption, but it is expected to contribute less
than previous years to overall growth amid weaker
labor income especially for middle-class consumers.

Inflation is expected to remain within target, though
food and energy price volatility present recurring
challenges. Downside risks include further softening of
real wages that could dampen consumption further,
heightened global trade tensions, and reversal of
external financial conditions. Domestically, weakerthan-planned revenues could test adherence to fiscal
rules and constrain spending. While upside potential
risk arises from stronger demand in major trading
partners and accelerated implementation of ongoing
business, trade and investment deregulation reforms.

A calibrated policy package can address near-term
pressures while lifting medium-term prospects.

Business enabling policies, which reverse declining
productivity growth and mobilize private capital into
high-value investment, remain core to creating good

jobs. In tandem, supply-side measures can start
upskilling the future labor force. Targeted interventions
to ease skills mismatches and improve job quality are
essential: strengthening labor market information
systems, expanding active labor market policies with
a focus on lifelong learning and digital upskilling, and
improving school-to-work transitions—especially for
youth and women—would raise employability and
support middle-class job creation. On fiscal policy, a
bolder revenue strategy—centered on quick wins in
tax administration and digitalization, broadening the
tax base through adjusted thresholds, and enhanced
compliance—can create fiscal space to expand
growth programs in infrastructure, human capital, and
climate resilience. On finance, well-designed, timebound credit guarantees and risk-sharing facilities,
underpinned by robust digital credit infrastructure, can
catalyze lending to viable MSMEs and tradable sectors,
help translate monetary easing into investment, and
crowd in private capital with modest fiscal outlays.

Taken together, these measures can sustain
Indonesia’s resilience and gradually lift potential
growth, ensuring that more households experience
tangible welfare gains as the economy navigates a
more uncertain global environment.


### II. Digital Foundations for Growth
Indonesia’s digital infrastructure—the foundation
of its emerging digital economy—has expanded
rapidly, but its quality has not kept pace with
rising demand and its usage remains uneven.

Internet access has expanded quickly: 72.8 percent
of Indonesians used the internet in 2024, up from 54
percent in 2020. Yet connectivity remains “broad but
shallow.” The domestic broadband backbone now
reaches 98 percent of districts, but only half of villages
have fiber optic distribution points. Eastern Indonesia
and some rural areas elsewhere mostly rely on costly
and capacity-constrained microwave or satellite links.

Performance lags far behind regional peers: Indonesia
ranks last among ASEAN upper middle-income
economies in fixed broadband penetration and in both
mobile and fixed broadband speeds. Just 22 percent of
schools have a 100 Mbps or faster internet connection

and 24 percent of health clinics report having good
internet, hampering government efforts to strengthen
education and healthcare service delivery. The result
is a growing divide between a digital Indonesia—
connected but constrained—and an unconnected
Indonesia that risks being left further behind.

Achieving near-universal mobile network coverage
in Indonesia has been a success story but mobile
internet speeds are among the slowest in ASEAN.

Tower sharing reforms and strong competition
have enabled 4G coverage above 99 percent of the
population and some of the region’s most affordable
prices. Yet internet speeds and reliability are poor
because Indonesia assigns barely half as much spectrum
for mobile internet as its neighbors—ranking last in the
G20—and has been delayed in release new bands. As


<!-- page 12 (printed p. 3) -->

a result, Indonesia’s 5G transition has been among the
slowest in the region’s major economies, which limits
the quality, energy and cost-efficiency gains that 5G can
deliver. Indonesia also maintains some of the region’s
highest spectrum fees—about 11 percent of mobile
network operator (MNO) revenues—limiting resources
available for network upgrades. Reduced fees could be
tied to clearer coverage and performance obligations.

In addition to fair allocation of more spectrum to
MNOs, improving quality of mobile broadband will
eventually also require accelerating tower fiberization,
given rising data traffic and the limits of satellite and
microwave links that connect many towers in rural
areas.

Fixed broadband remains Indonesia’s most
significant
connectivity
weakness,
limiting
productivity gains. Only one-fifth of households
have a fixed broadband connection. Coverage is
concentrated in urban areas, as high deployment
costs, complex permitting, and limited infrastructure
sharing slow expansion to rural areas. Telkom’s twothirds market share and the difficulty smaller internet
service providers face in accessing affordable backbone
capacity reduce competition, keeping prices high and
quality of service low. As a result, fixed broadband
remains unaffordable for many households, costing
about twice as much relative to income as in Malaysia,
Thailand, and Viet Nam. Telkom’s planned spinoff of
its fiber assets into a wholesale subsidiary offers an
opportunity to move toward a more open, shared
national backbone and strengthen competition across
the sector.

Reducing the high cost of rural fiber deployment
will require more infrastructure sharing—including
access to PLN’s poles—and complementary use
of fixed wireless access (FWA) and satellite. In
eastern Indonesia, high backbone costs and weak
interconnections across networks, especially on the
Palapa Ring, keep internet prices elevated and reduce
resilience. The recent release of FWA spectrum and
announced plans to offer packages at less than half
of current averages could support wider uptake,
though inherent capacity limits mean FWA will likely
play a transitional role and complement—rather than
replace—the need for a national fiber network. To
maximize impact, the government’s SATRIA-1 satellite
should be focused on the most remote areas of
Indonesia—especially in eastern Indonesia—where

neither fiber nor FWA is feasible. Satellites also provide
critical redundancy during disasters or network outages,
making targeted use even more important. Ensuring
the financial sustainability of BAKTI—the government’s
universal service agency—by exploring a shift from
operating networks to acting as a market enabler will
also be important for extending connectivity to remote
areas.

Indonesia’s data center capacity is expanding, but
growth is constrained by a complex investment
environment. Local data centers matter because
data-intensive services and newer AI applications
need computing capacity close to users. They also
attract investment, support technology transfer and
local innovation, and create skilled digital jobs. Despite
being ASEAN’s largest digital economy, Indonesia
trails Malaysia and Singapore by a wide margin in
data center capacity—Malaysia has around twenty
times more capacity per capita—and growth in 2025
was outpaced by Thailand. Relatively slow approval
processes, complex and uncoordinated permitting,
uncertainty over data governance, and challenges in
securing reliable and renewable electricity and water
connections deter investors. Greater adoption of
cloud by the public sector and Danantara’s interest
in sustainable data centers offer timely opportunities
for the government to mobilize private capital and

A. Economic Update
### 1. Recent Economic Developments
Indonesia’s economy has been resilient despite
global uncertainty, with steady growth driven
by investment and commodities exports, but
consumption is softening amid souring perceptions
of economic performance.

The global economy in 2025 faced heightened
policy uncertainty, and elevated geopolitical and
trade tensions. Stockpiling, supply-chain adjustments,
and easing global financing conditions have partially
cushioned economic activity from these shocks. In East
Asia and the Pacific (EAP), economic activity remains
strong, supported by robust trade, tourism, and
consumption. But, as some of these temporary benefits
of accelerated trade flow and favorable financing
subside, and as tariffs are passed through to end
consumers, global growth is expected to moderate.

Greater trade barriers externally, as well as limited
structural reform domestically present challenges
to create better jobs in EAP, while automation and
Artificial Intelligence (AI) are rapidly changing the
shape of work (EAP Economic Update, October 2025).

Amid this uncertainty, Indonesia’s economy has
remained resilient, buoyed by investment and
net exports. GDP growth reached 5.0 percent yearon-year (yoy) in the first three quarters of 2025 (3Q25), at par with recent years (Figure A.1). Domestic
demand remains the main growth driver, although
the contribution of net exports rose from -0.1 in 2024
to 21 percent, marking its greatest contribution for
five years. This effect is temporary though as it was
driven by front-loaded goods exports and rising global
demand for commodities, particularly palm oil, iron
steel, and gold. Private consumption growth inched
down, contributing 2.7 percentage points (pp) to GDP
(53.3 percent of GDP growth), down from 2.8 pp in

2024 (54.8 percent of GDP growth). Low core inflation,
sluggish car and retail sales, and subdued credit
growth all point to moderating private consumption.

Public consumption’s contribution to growth dropped
sharply from 0.5 pp in 2024 to 0.1 pp in 2025 (9.6 to 1.9
percent of GDP growth), due to budget reallocations
and slow disbursement in priority programs. In line
with robust capital goods imports, investment remains
strong, contributing 1.4 pp to growth (29 percent of
GDP growth), unchanged from 2024, driven by public
defense and industrial sectors.

Growth is relatively broad based, with services
supported by fiscal stimulus and agriculture by
favorable climate conditions. Services contributed
57.4 percent to growth in 3Q-25, up from 56.2 percent
in 2024, led by education, health, business services,
and ICT (Figure A.2). Services growth was boosted
by public spending on flagship education and health
programs. Fiscal stimulus also benefited wholesale,
retail, and hospitality sectors including leisure activities
in tourist-led regions such as Bali and Nusa Tenggara.

Manufacturing also performed relatively well, led
by the basic metal, pharmaceutical, and food and
beverage industries. Manufacturing benefited from
external demand for palm oil products, chemical
materials, and iron and steel products. Those three
sub-sectors’ contribution rose by 2.3 pp from last year
to 15.7 percent. Agriculture output hit a decade high
thanks to favorable climate conditions. In addition,
private investment in palm oil (CPO) production and
downstreaming accelerated. Growth in the mining
sector was subdued, affected by lower coal exports
due to rising production in China and India and the
suspension of PT Freeport Indonesia’s operations
following a landslide at the Grasberg Mine in Papua.

#### Box A.1: Inflation and Rice Food Policy
In 2025, food inflation was partly affected by policies that increased rice prices. BULOG is mandated to secure
national food availability and price stability by conducting procurement, storage, and distribution of key staples,
especially rice. It also carries out government-assigned tasks such as managing public food reserves, implementing
market operations to stabilize prices, and supporting food assistance programs for vulnerable households. In 2025,
there was a major policy shift in Indonesia’s food trade system. To boost farmers’ income and ensure high absorption
during harvest season, the government mandated BULOG to purchase all unhusked rice (gabah) at a higher price
of IDR6,500 per kilogram, regardless of quality. Previously, BULOG would offer reduced prices (i.e., price deductions
(rafaksi)) for lower quality rice and could also opt not to purchase any additional quantities. High crop production
coupled with the new policy resulted in BULOG’s rice stocks surging to a record 4.2 million tons by July, up from 1.7
million tons in January, and remaining through October.

This policy imposes a considerable fiscal cost. In 2025, BULOG’s allocation for procuring rice reached 0.07 percent
of GDP (IDR16.6 trillion). This figure accounts for 11.5 percent of the government’s total food security budget for 2025.
For comparison, BULOG’s allocation is equivalent to around56 percent of the Ministry of Agriculture’s (MOA) overall
budget. By early September, all the allocation for BULOG’s rice procurement had been fully disbursed.
A rice surplus does not automatically ensure price stability. Rice, which accounts for 12.2 percent of the food and
beverages consumption basket, is a key inflation driver for Indonesia. Despite a five-year high production of 35 million
tons, resulting in rice self-sufficiency, food prices have steadily increased since February. By September, rice prices in 214
districts surpassed the government’s Retail Price Ceiling. As a result, the Ministry of Home Affairs (MOHA) instructed local
leaders to step up inflation monitoring, provide rice aid, and accelerate rice supply distribution operations (Stabilisasi
Pasokan dan Harga Pangan, SPHP) to curb further price pressures.

However, policies aimed at stabilizing prices placed strain on distribution channels with unintended consequences
of exacerbating food price inflation. Narrowing margins between the Government Purchase Price (Harga Pembelian
Pemerintah, HPP or farmgate prices) and the regulated Retail Prices Ceiling (Harga Eceran Tertinggi, HET), coupled with
high logistics costs, are discouraging rice movement and affecting small and medium mills, resulting in slower private
supply. In addition, uncertainty regarding revised quality classifications and potential new ceiling prices is leading
producers and distributors to delay procurement or retain stocks. This further restricts market supply.
These policies may also lead to greater risk of stock spoilage. Rising rice stockpiles has created new challenges
like warehouse capacity constraints and quality issues. Greater variation in rice quality, such as increased moisture and
impurity levels, increases the risk of spoilage during storage. The MOA has reported that a large amount of rice in
the warehouses has been damaged due to warehouses’ overcapacity and distribution bottlenecks. Addressing issues
related to price controls at the various levels of the supply chain coupled with tackling bottlenecks are important to
ensure a smooth supply of rice and align incentives of farmers, distributors, and consumers.

#### Box A.2: Listening to Indonesia survey
Listening to Indonesia (L2INDO) is a nationally representative, high-frequency phone survey conducted monthly
since March 2024. L2INDO is a joint Bappenas-World Bank program that tracks household welfare, economic
conditions, and public sentiment through a nationally representative monthly panel survey since March 2024. This
survey tracks the social and economic wellbeing of households across Indonesia, capturing public views on economic
conditions and policy with only a short lag. By blending welfare monitoring with polling-style questions, L2INDO enables
a closer examination of the drivers behind changing conditions and perceptions. The survey gathers perspectives from
people from different socio-economic backgrounds and regions providing a comprehensive view of how households
experience and respond to economic and policy shifts.

Each month, the survey aims to interview up to 2,000 households, randomly selected from the first baseline
survey of 5,000-household conducted in October–November 2023 across 500 communities. The same households
are contacted in every round, with replacements drawn from the same community when respondents drop out. As of
today, over 80 percent of current participants have completed more than 15 rounds. A second baseline, conducted in
July–September 2025 with the same sample size, will further expand the sample pool.

Each round includes core modules on economic and social vulnerability, economic activities, income, financial
behavior, and perceptions of economic conditions and governance. Rotating additional modules cover digital
activities, subsidy views, coping strategies, trust in taxation and governance, and other emerging topics such as housing,
environmental conditions, and inequality. The second baseline also includes Indonesia’s first national measure of lead
(Pb) exposure, indoor air quality monitoring and time use.

The survey is expected to catalyze change by aligning development strategies with public views and concerns, thereby
strengthening the social contract through the integration of public sentiment into policy formulation. It also aims to
address knowledge gaps related to volatility and vulnerability.

2025, the non-performing loans (NPL) ratio remains low
at 2.3 percent of total loans. The capital adequacy ratio
(CAR) stood at 26 percent, well above the regulatory
minimum of 10.5 percent. The level of provisioning
was 175 percent of the value of NPLs, providing ample
loss-absorption capacity. The loan at risk (LAR) ratio8
stands at 9.7 percent, less than half its level during
pandemic years (Figure A.12). A reverse stress test9
on 136 Indonesian banks using balance sheet data
suggests that the system can withstand a significant
credit shock: NPLs would need to rise to 18 percent
of total loans before the weakest 20 percent of banks
fall below minimum capital adequacy. This “distance to
distress” remained consistently high during and after
the pandemic.

amid potentially weak demand for credit and a
risks mismatch between demand and supply. As of

respectively, a slight uptick over the last couple

### 2. The Policy Stance
Fiscal stimulus to support domestic demand is
constrained by declining revenue and underspending.

The fiscal deficit widened as revenue weakened
amid structural constraints. The fiscal deficit reached
2.0 percent of GDP as of October 2025, 0.6 pp higher
than the same period last year (Figure A.16). Revenue
dropped to 8.9 percent of GDP, down 1.3 pp from
October 2024.12 Three factors drove this decline. First,
easing commodity prices reduced related revenues.13
Second, the revised tax refund mechanism accelerated
refunds and led to a contraction in net tax collections
(MOF 2025a) (Figure A.17).14,15 Third, State-Owned
Enterprises (SOEs) dividends, equivalent to 0.3 percent
of GDP, were transferred to Danantara16 in March 2025.

Public spending as a share of GDP declined to
accommodate the falling revenue envelope. Central
government spending (including subnational transfers)
fell by 0.7 pp of GDP compared to last year. This
contraction was broad-based (Figure A.18), reflecting
budget efficiency measures implemented in early
2025.17 There has also been underspending in some
priority programs (Figure A.19).18 Interest payments,
at 1.8 percent of GDP as of October (21 percent of
total revenue), have remained high and at par with

the previous year. At the subnational level, although
transfers declined only slightly, actual spending
contracted by 7 percent yoy, likely due to changes
in local leadership and budget efficiency measures
introduced in early 202519 (MOF 2025b). Meanwhile,
the government delivered quarterly fiscal stimulus
targeting social assistance and worker incentives to
support domestic demand (see Box A.3).

Budget financing conditions have remained
favorable. With a larger deficit, net financing
requirements reached 86 percent of the budget target
by October 2025, up from 73 percent in the same period
last year. Government bond yields fell across all tenors
amid monetary policy easing. The 10-year benchmark
yield dropped to 6.07 percent in late October 2025,
its lowest since mid-2013, before rebounding slightly
to 6.25 in late November. Investor demand remained
strong, with the bid-to-cover ratio rising to 4.6 in
October from 1.8 a year earlier. The demand is mostly
from domestic investors, most notably commercial
banks, insurance companies, pension funds, and mutual
funds. Nonresident investors, however, recorded net
outflows in September to November, reducing yearto-date inflows and their share in tradable bonds.20

#### Box A.3: 2025 Fiscal Stimulus
In 2025, the government introduced a fiscal stimulus package focusing on boosting household consumption and
providing labor-market incentives to stimulate job creation. The stimulus amounts to 0.5 percent of GDP (Figure A).
The measures are grouped into six major categories (Figure B): social transfers, worker incentives, auto-sector incentives,
housing-sector incentives, MSME tax incentives, and other programs.

Support to stimulate consumption is delivered through social transfers and targeted incentives in priority sectors.
Social transfers (0.28 percent of GDP) include cash transfers, rice aid, and electricity-tariff discount for low-income
households. Measures also included discounts on public transports tickets as well as tickets for trains, flights, and ferries.
Sector incentives included VAT subsidy on automobile purchases (0.06 percent of GDP) and housing (0.02 percent of GDP);
as well as luxury goods tax reduction and zero percent import duty for EVs.

Labor-market incentives focus on workers, MSMEs, and employers. The worker-incentive programs (0.08 percent of
GDP) include easier access to Job Loss Insurance (JKP), wage subsidies for low-income workers and contract teachers, cash
for-work daily-wage jobs, and six-months paid internship programs for 100,000 recent graduates to help provide work
experience and improve employability. Tax incentives include an income tax waiver to reduce labor costs and temporary
income tax relief for the tourism sector. Meanwhile, MSMEs continue to benefit from the 0.5 percent final income tax,
with full exemption for very small firms, while employers receive interest-subsidy support for machinery revitalization and
temporary reductions in workplace-accident premiums (JKK).

Monetary easing has progressed, but muted demand
and tradeoff between growth and external pressures
constrain its impact.

Low inflation has enabled BI to maintain an
accommodative policy stance, but monetary easing
has had limited pass-through so far. Since September
2024, BI has cut its policy rate by 150 bps, from 6.25
percent to 4.75 percent as of October 2025, the lowest
level since 2022. Despite those cuts, average bank
deposit and lending rates have only declined by 83
and 15 bps, respectively (Figure A.22). This is in part
due to the special (higher) rates provided to large
depositors, which account for 26 percent of banks’ total
third-party funds. Moreover, the limited pass-through
suggests friction in monetary transmission—stemming
from shallow financial markets, prevailing informality,
and price controls—which limit the domestic response
of policy rate adjustments (see IEP December 2023).

Concurrently, BI has reduced the issuance of short-term
monetary securities (SRBI), lowering their outstanding
stock by 0.9 percent of GDP year-to-September. This
policy also reflects a more accommodative stance
aimed at boosting liquidity. As a result, broad money
(M2) growth has picked up recently (Figure A.23), but it
was mostly led by higher net foreign assets (NFA) from
trade surpluses.

BI is utilizing macroprudential incentives to further
improve monetary policy transmission. Starting in
December, BI will introduce two policy changes for its
Reserve Requirement Ratio (RRR) incentive scheme.

First, it will change the criteria for those benefiting
from the 5 pp RRR reduction. Eligibility will now require
banks’ commitment to future lending in targeted
sectors rather than realized loan growth. Second, it will
provide an additional cut of up to 0.5 pp, on top of
the existing 5 pp reduction, depending on how quickly
banks lower lending rates relative to BI rate cuts.

Commercial banks with a lending rate cut elasticity of
0.3–0.6 will get an additional 0.4 pp RRR reduction,
while those above 0.6 elasticity receive an extra 0.5 pp
RRR cut (Table A.1). This new incentive is expected to
unlock IDR123 trillion of liquidity (1.5 percent of credit
to the private sector). In implementing these incentives,
BI and OJK will periodically assess banks’ credit growth
commitments and their realization.

### 3. Outlook and Risks
Indonesia’s economy is projected to remain
resilient. Growth is projected at 5.0 percent per year
in 2025-26, rising to 5.2 percent in 2027 (Table A.2).

Private consumption will contribute on average 53
percent of overall GDP growth, less than previous
years, amid weaknesses in labor income. This is
expected to be partially offset by low inflation and
targeted fiscal stimulus that could boost consumption.

Investment growth is projected to gradually increase,
averaging 6.2 percent between 2025 and 2027. This
will be supported by three drivers: a scale up of stateled investment via Danantara23 (Table A.3), further
monetary easing to stimulate private sector credit,

and FDI through downstreaming, deregulation, and
special economic zones reform targeting energy,
natural resources, manufacturing, and services.24
Rising investment should offset weaker net-exports
contributions, which are estimated to average 5.5
percent as terms-of-trade decline. Commodity
based manufacturing, agribusiness, construction,
and services are projected to drive the supply-side,
reflecting government priorities. Rising investment
levels are expected to boost imports and contribute to
a widening current account deficit (up to 1.3 percent of
GDP in 2027), which is expected to be financed through
FDI and portfolio flows. Gross international reserve

The risks to the outlook are balanced. On the

the implementation of Indonesia’s new trade deals

### 4. Policy Priorities
Strengthening active labor market programs
and information systems as well as investing in
foundational skills and teaching quality is key to
addressing job polarization and skills mismatch.

Indonesia has experienced 5 decades of strong
growth reaching Upper Middle-Income in 2023.

Growth has delivered on jobs and higher living
standards. The country now faces challenges common
to Middle-Income Countries. Growth is stagnant (at
around 5 percent) when it needs to accelerate to
achieve high income by 2045, and the economy is
struggling to create good and middle-class paying
jobs. The demand for these jobs will come through
policies that reverse the decline in productivity growth.

As such, a stronger push is necessary on business
enabling policies and predictability of the regulatory
environment. This includes reforms to deregulate,
promote competition, support firm entry-exit, and
encourage quality investment to expand good jobs.

Moreover, mobilizing private capital will be key.

Business enabling policies should boost higher-value
investment, requiring more private capital to support
businesses with loans, equity, guarantees, and risk
insurance (World Bank Indonesia Country Growth and
Jobs Report, forthcoming).30 While these reforms focus
on the binding constraints for boosting the demand
for good jobs, it is also important to start preparing
supply-side interventions to boost the skills of the
future labor force.

Supply-side interventions are also needed to
improve the quality of jobs. Globalization, greening,
and technology have changed the location, content,
and structure of work resulting in rapid shifts in demand
for occupations and skills. Across the world, routine
manual, and cognitive skills—abundant in Indonesia—
are facing declining demand. Instead, advanced
cognitive skills such as problem-solving and verbal
ability acquired through strong foundational education
are facing increasing demand, as technical skills
learned through the TVET (Technical and Vocational
Education and Training) system, universities, and onthe-job training. Further, Indonesia will be aging in
the next decade, and the cohorts entering working
age is forecast to start shrinking in the early 2030s.

Policies that encourage women to enter the labor
force and remain active during prime age together
with improved labor productivity, will be needed to
continue to accelerate economic growth.


Indonesia can adopt a combination of short-andmedium term supply-side interventions to address
growing job polarization and skill mismatches.

Short-term:

a. Address information failures to support
optimal decision making by individuals and
firms through the development of a worldclass labor market information system.

Evidence shows that better allocation of talent
across occupations leads to economic growth and
higher individual earnings.31 Providing information
on the labor market returns of different fields can
also reduce the gender imbalance in career and
college major choices.32 As such, a modernized
Labor Market Information System (LMIS) and
robust Public Employment Services (PES) can
significantly reduce frictional unemployment by
lowering search costs and improving matching
quality. Evidence suggests that effective PES
can increase the exit rate from unemployment
by streamlining vacancy referrals and reducing
mismatch (refer to World Bank, 2020; Card et al.,
2010) especially at the lower end of middle-skilled
job matches where private sector employment
services might be less utilized. Strengthening
Indonesia’s digital employment platforms (e.g.,
KarirHub) to provide real-time signaling on skills
demand—rather than just job listings—will help
align the education system’s output with market
realities and minimize the “scarring effects” of
early-career unemployment (see ILO, 2024;
Hensvik et al, 2019).

b. Develop effective active labor market policies
(ALMPs) with a renewed focus on lifelong
learning. Evidence highlights that well-targeted
ALMPs—such as re-skilling and up-skilling
programs aligned with employer needs, career
counseling services based on real-time labor
market intelligence and wage subsidies for
displaced middle-skill workers—can facilitate
smoother transitions into emerging sectors
(ILO, 2021; OECD, 2023). Denmark’s flexicurity
model blends flexible labor laws with generous
unemployment benefits and publicly funded
upskilling, enabling rapid reallocation of workers
toward emerging job opportunities. Singapore
emphasizes lifelong learning and adaptability
through its SkillsFuture initiative, which offers

Short to Medium-term: It is vital to enhance
foundational skills to promote a future ready
workforce able to adapt to a rapidly evolving labor
market. Indonesia scores lower than regional peers on
foundational skills, with more than half of Indonesian
youth not meeting minimum proficiency standards

competencies, that are pivotal to future learning. Key
amongst effective approaches is investing in early

through structured pedagogy and scaffolding as well

Brazil, for example, show that significant improvements
in learning outcomes can be achieved with proven
education reforms that are underpinned by strong
political commitment and robust accountability.

Vietnam’s success derives from prioritizing foundational
learning at the national level, setting clear targets
for literacy and learning, investing in teacher quality
and leveraging assessment data to both improve
instruction and monitor results. In contrast, reform at
the municipal level in Sobral propelled it from one of
the lowest to the highest performing systems in the
country. This was spearheaded through a sequenced

set of reforms, structured pedagogy and a focus on
accountability where performance-based incentives
and regular monitoring of schools proved effective in
holding teachers and school leaders accountable for
student learning outcomes.

Those reforms and programs are largely brought by
and financed, but not exclusively, by the public sector;
and hence will require doubling down on current
government efforts to create fiscal space.

A bold revenues strategy coupled with ringfencing
growth-enhancing public spending can help
mitigate fiscal risks, boost fiscal space, and support
government growth ambitions.

Fiscal risks need to be considered going forward.

If revenue performance remains weak, the 2025 fiscal
deficit could approach the statutory limit under full
budget execution. Similar risks apply to the 2026
budget.33 Lower transfers to subnational governments—
down about 20 percent—could constrain service
delivery and affect local development outcomes.

Persistent revenue shortfalls and underperforming
transfers would limit fiscal space and test adherence to
deficit rules in the years ahead.

A bolder and more proactive revenue strategy
is needed to safeguard fiscal sustainability. With
rising risks of revenue shortfalls and wider deficits
in 2026-27, stronger revenue mobilization is urgent.

The government could focus on quick wins in
tax administration and digitalization. Expanding
e-services, enhancing data integration and simplifying
filing procedures can mobilize around 1 percent of
GDP in additional revenue, at least for VAT and CIT34.

Revenue could also be strengthened by lowering VAT
and corporate income tax (CIT) thresholds, which are
set significantly higher than in comparable countries.

Broader CIT bases—through phasing out reduced
rates, special regimes and other incentives—would
also enhance fairness and efficiency. Reducing VAT
exemptions in selected sectors could further improve
revenue performance while preserving neutrality.

These policy measures focusing on VAT and CIT could
raise up to an additional 1.5 percent of GDP. Finally,
tax reforms to impose standardized tax treatment on
capital gains could mobilize additional revenue in an
economically efficient way, while supporting greater
equality.

Greater emphasis on capital spending would raise the

boosting access to credit and encouraging bankable

Properly designed credit guarantees such as partial
credit guarantees36, portfolio guarantees, and first-loss
facilities reduce both expected losses and uncertainty
for lenders. By sharing risk, they enable banks to
extend credit to MSMEs that lack collateral or credit
history, offer larger loan sizes and longer tenors, and
ultimately make small-business lending commercially
viable.

Public-sector intervention through guarantees
is often justified where information asymmetries
and market failures constrain credit allocation.

Guarantees diversify risks more efficiently than
individual lenders and mobilize private capital at a
fraction of the fiscal cost of direct lending. Channeling
public resources into partial credit guarantees is
typically less costly than providing direct liquidity while
maintaining strong incentives for prudent lending

and borrower discipline. Guarantees mobilize private
capital without requiring full public funding up front,
and they mobilize private capital rather than replacing
it. As a result, well-designed guarantee schemes can
catalyze substantial private investment with relatively
modest public budgets.

The
government-funded
credit
guarantee
program—KUR—plays a central role in expanding
MSME finance. The People’s Business Credit (Kredit
Usaha Rakyat, KUR) program has been instrumental in
unlocking credit to the unbanked and underbanked as
evidenced by more than 85 percent of KUR borrowers
are first-time recipients of formal credit. The KUR
program funds the guarantee fees and subsidizes
interest rates while leaving loan capital provision to
financial institutions. The credit guarantee is provided by
OJK-licensed public and private guarantors companies
for a fee. However, enhancing graduation incentives,
targeting rules, and enforcement of inclusivity criteria
is essential to reduce long-term subsidy dependence
and ensure the program consistently reaches priority
segments. Guarantee institutions also need clearer
mandates and product innovation to expand their risk
appetite beyond government programs such as KUR
and support more complex, productivity-enhancing
investments.

Guarantee programs are most effective when
anchored in strong digital credit infrastructure.

Indonesia has made progress with expanding
number of credit reporting service providers, but
further harmonization, data-sharing reciprocity, and
integration of alternative data are needed. A robust open
finance framework—linking payment data, utilities,
business registry information, land certification, and
the movable collateral (fidusia) registry—can sharply
improve credit scoring accuracy, reduce borrowing
costs, and unlock access to a wider range of financial
services. Complementary reforms to insolvency, debt
resolution, creditor rights, and the fidusia framework
will further strengthen lender confidence and expand
the pool of bankable MSMEs.

Together, well-designed guarantees and modern credit
infrastructure form a mutually reinforcing foundation
for Indonesia’s digital financial ecosystem—mobilizing
private capital, boosting productivity, and supporting
inclusive, sustainable growth.

B. Digital Foundations for Growth
### 1. The Imperative to Expand and Upgrade Indonesia’s Digital Infrastructure
Indonesia can transform widespread digital access
into increased productivity and better-quality jobs
by improving the quality, reliability, and reach of its
digital infrastructure. Digital technologies can have
important impacts on how economies grow, firms
compete, governments function, countries respond
to shocks, and how people work, learn, communicate,
and access information. But realizing their full potential
depends on the foundations of fast, reliable, and
affordable internet and modern data centers with
cloud capabilities, for which international evidence
shows substantial gains in productivity, employment,
and economic growth (see Box B.1). Improved
connectivity enables broader industrial upgrading to
support automation, advanced manufacturing, and
more digital services. Artificial intelligence (AI), for
example, can only generate productivity gains where
reliable broadband and data center capacity allow data
to move and be processed efficiently. For Indonesia,
stronger digital infrastructure can play a key role in
driving the next generation of economic growth—
anchored in higher productivity, greater resilience, and
more inclusive participation in the digital economy.

Internet use in Indonesia has expanded rapidly—
from 54 percent of the population in 2020 to 72.8
percent in 202437—yet connectivity remains “broad
but shallow”, with speeds and modes of access
limiting how productively it can be used. Despite
the progress, internet use in Indonesia is behind the
ASEAN and upper-middle-income countries averages
(75 and 81 percent, respectively) (Figure B.1).38 Strong
competition and infrastructure sharing have helped
expand mobile coverage to almost all populated areas
and kept data affordable—a remarkable achievement

given Indonesia’s geography. By contrast, weaker
competition and fragmented infrastructure in fixed
broadband have constrained coverage, quality, and
affordability: only 22 percent of households39 have fixed
broadband connections and 22 percent of schools have
100+ Mbps internet connections.40 Indonesia continues
to significantly lag regional and income-level peers
on average broadband speeds. Furthermore, only 10
and 3 percent of Indonesians use a laptop or desktop
computer,41 compared with 26 percent in Thailand and
80 percent in Malaysia,42 limiting engagement in more
complex or data-intensive activities.

#### Box B.1: Summary of empirical evidence on the economic benefits of better internet
There is a substantial and growing body of evidence linking improvements in broadband coverage and speed to
positive economic and social outcomes. A 2024 global meta-review concluded that higher broadband availability and
quality typically raise GDP and productivity.45 Relevant recent empirical studies include:

•

A 2019 firm-level study of 20 European Union (EU) countries (2010-2015) found that a 10-percentage-point
increase in broadband adoption raises total factor productivity by 3.5-5.8 percent over five years, with particularly
strong effects in manufacturing and digital-intensive sectors.46

•

A 2020 study of Turkish manufacturing firms (2012-2015) found that improving broadband speeds above 100
Mbps led to a 5-6 percentage point increase in firm-level productivity.47

•

A 2021 ITU study of 139 countries (2010-2020) found that 10 percent rise in fixed broadband penetration increased
GDP per capita by 0.85 percent in middle-income and 1.25 percent in high-income countries.48 A 2025 update
covering 107 countries (2010-2023) shows stronger effects at 1.29 and 1.43 percent respectively, with even higher
gains in Asia Pacific rising from 1.53 to 1.84 percent.49

•

A 2022 cross-country study of 116 economies (2014-2019) found that a 10 percentage-point increase in mobilebroadband speed is associated with about 0.2 percentage-point higher labor productivity in the following year,
based on fixed-effects panel estimations.50

•

A 2022 firm-level study (2006-2018) analyzing the staggered 2014-2016 “Broadband China” rollout as a quasinatural experiment found that broadband expansion raised firm productivity by 4 percent.51

•

A 2023 study in Peru found that connecting schools to broadband led to gradually rising student test scores,
from near zero impact in the first year to statistically significant learning gains after five years.52

There is comparatively less evidence related to increased data center capacity. However, a 2022 study using crosscountry data found that a 10 percent increase in data center capacity raises exports of data-related services by about
1.6 percent, while a similar increase in secure internet servers raises such exports by 2.1 percent—highlighting the
importance of digital infrastructure for competitiveness in global services trade.53

#### Box B.2: Digital financial services: The next growth frontier in Indonesia
Indonesia is well positioned for the next phase of digital financial innovation, but realizing this potential will require a
more integrated and interoperable ecosystem. Fragmentation across platforms and bilateral APIs continue to limit scale.
The National Standard for Payment Open API (SNAP) has made progress but could further evolve into a full-fledge Open
Finance framework covering more services such as savings, credit and insurance, to help households and firms manage
risk and build resilience. A structured Open Finance framework, which enables consumer-consented data sharing under
robust governance and protection could unlock innovation such as data-driven credit scoring and alternative data use.
International experience shows that harmonization and interoperability are prerequisites for scaling digital payments,
supporting cross-border transactions, and integrating Indonesia into regional and global value chains.
Improving credit infrastructure is also critical. Strengthening the secured transaction regulatory framework and development
of the moveable collateral registry (fidusia) would unlock lending for MSMEs and households, while harmonization
and interoperability of existing credit reporting systems—to expand data coverage for borrowers’ creditworthiness
assessment—could be a “quick win” with significant impact.

The next frontier for Indonesia’s digital financial services lies in harnessing advanced technologies—particularly AI, open
finance, and cross-border payment innovations—to drive inclusion, efficiency, and trade competitiveness. The Financial
Services Authority (OJK) and Bank Indonesia (BI) are piloting AI and machine learning tools to enhance supervisory
technology (SupTech), risk-based supervision, and consumer protection, with ongoing World Bank support for scaling
these solutions. On the trade front, BI’s Project Garuda is piloting a central bank digital currency (CBDC)—the Digital
Rupiah—designed to support programmable, cross-border payments and integration with regional payment systems,
lowering transaction costs and facilitating MSME trade. To realize these gains, Indonesia must harmonize regulatory
frameworks, ensure interoperability across platforms, and strengthen financial consumer protection and anti-scam
frameworks to foster public trust and confidence.

Indonesia’s digital public infrastructure (DPI)65 is
taking shape, but adoption needs to accelerate to
deliver broader impact. As connectivity improves,
Indonesia has an opportunity to build inclusive
and dynamic digital identity, digital payments, data
sharing, and other foundational platforms that can
underpin seamless service delivery, expand economic
participation, and accelerate the country’s digital

assistance delivery in Banyuwangi regency that will be
scaled nationally in 2026. This “use case” approach—
focused on solving real problems—offers a practical
model for aligning institutions and accelerating

### 3. Strengthening Indonesia’s Digital Infrastructure
Digital infrastructure underpins how data moves,
is processed, and ultimately reaches people and
firms—connecting Indonesia to the world and
carrying data across its vast archipelago. Such
infrastructure can be viewed as a value chain that runs
from the first mile—international connectivity—to the
last mile, where households, schools, and firms go
online. Indonesia’s first mile connectivity is through
submarine cables, before feeding into national
backbones that carry data across islands through the
middle mile. These domestic networks—combining
terrestrial fiber, microwave, and satellite links—connect
cities, towns, and villages across the archipelago
to Internet Exchange Points (IXPs) and data centers,
where data is cached and processed locally to improve

performance and reliability, as well as to reduce costs
of data transit from overseas. Each step in this chain
involves infrastructure and interconnection costs that
are ultimately reflected in what users pay for internet
access. Finally, the last mile delivers access through
fiber-to-the-home or -premise (FTTH/FTTP), and
through towers that provide mobile broadband and
fixed wireless access (FWA) over dedicated spectrum
frequencies, complemented by satellite connections
that enable reach to the most remote communities.

Given Indonesia’s geography of more than 17,000
islands, with sharp differences in terrain, density, and
income that shape network economics, extending this
infrastructure is costly and complex.

Backbone
Indonesia’s
international
connectivity—the
first mile—has expanded rapidly but remains
concentrated. The vast majority of Indonesia’s
international bandwidth is routed from Singapore
through landing stations in Batam, creating a singlecorridor dependency that raises costs and exposes
the network to congestion and potential disruptions,
including natural hazards. Several new submarine
cable systems—including recent routes deployed by
hyperscale cloud providers—now pass near Indonesia
without landing, possibly reflecting concerns about
permitting complexity and route restrictions. This
reduces Indonesia’s direct connectivity to global
networks and reinforces dependence on the
Singapore-Batam corridor. A recent Minister of
Maritime Affairs and Fisheries Decree (KepMenKKP No.


14 of 2021) reinforces this concentration by restricting
new international cables to Batam, Manado, Jayapura,
and Kupang. The exclusion of Jakarta, the country’s
commercial and data center hub, limits investment
in data-intensive industries that depend on lowlatency links. A Ministry of Transportation regulation
(Permenhub No. 2 of 2021), based on cabotage rules
in Government Regulations No. 20 of 2010 and No.

22 of 2011, also restricts foreign-flagged vessels from
undertaking submarine cable repairs, requiring special
permits even though there are limited Indonesianflagged options.80 Allowing additional landing sites—
especially Jakarta but also other commercial hubs
such as Makassar, Medan, and Surabaya—would
strengthen resilience, attract data center investment,
and lower transit costs to rural and eastern Indonesia.81
Streamlining approvals through a single window
licensing process and allowing foreign repair vessels
would further enhance competitiveness.

Indonesia’s domestic backbone now reaches
nearly all cities and districts, but large gaps in
middle-mile connectivity, especially to villages,
continue to limit rural access and service quality.

Fiber ODPs exist in 98 percent of cities and districts,
but only 70 percent of kecamatan (sub-districts)
and half of villages.82 The backbone is dominated
by Telkom, complemented by PLN’s fiber, the Palapa
Ring, and a handful of private operators. The lack of
integration into a neutral or shared backbone has
led to duplication along profitable routes and weak
coverage in less-commercial regions. Large parts of
rural and eastern Indonesia still depend on microwave
or satellite backhaul, which are more expensive, less
reliable and have less capacity than fiber, leading to
slower speeds, higher latency, and higher retail prices
for users, as well as disruption in poor weather. Closing
these middle mile gaps and improving interconnection
among backbone providers will be essential to lower
costs and strengthen competition.

Mobile broadband
Indonesia’s mobile broadband market is one of the
most competitive in ASEAN, enabling widespread
coverage and among the region’s most affordable
prices. There are now three mobile network operators
(MNOs)—Telkomsel, Indosat, and XLSmart85—following
a period of consolidation and market rationalization.

Strong competition, reinforced by regulations and
policies that mandated tower sharing and opened the
market to independent tower companies (see Box B.3),
has helped to push 4G coverage above 99 percent
of the population and driven retail prices down to
around 0.6 percent of GNI per capita—well below
the International Telecommunications Union (ITU)
affordability target of 2 percent. Introducing mobile
number portability would further strengthen consumer
choice and competitive pressure, empowering users to
switch MNOs more easily without changing numbers.86
As noted in the ‘Institutional Arrangements’ section,
public investment through BAKTI has complemented
private rollout by connecting non-commercial areas,
notwithstanding BAKTI’s sustainability challenges.



#### Box B.3: The success and lesson of Indonesia’s tower sharing reforms
Indonesia’s mobile network tower sharing framework was established through Kominfo Ministerial Regulation No. 2
of 2008, which required operators to share passive infrastructure and allowed independent tower companies to build
and lease towers to multiple tenants. This regulation was later replaced by Joint Regulation of Minister of Home
Affairs, Minister of Public Works, Minister of Kominfo, and Head of BKPM (No. 18 of 2009, 07/PRT/M/2009, 19 of 2009,
3 of 2009). Implementation was uneven at first, but enforcement strengthened in the late 2010s as Kominfo began
conditioning new site permits on evidence of sharing. Further reforms under the Omnibus Law on Job Creation (Law
No. 11 of 2020) implemented through Presidential Regulation No. 10 of 2021 on the New Investment List liberalized the
telecommunications tower sector, allowing full foreign ownership and investment.

These reforms curbed duplication, lowered capital costs, and created one of the region’s largest neutral-host tower
markets. Today, over 90 percent of towers are owned by tower companies, providing a more efficient backbone for
mobile network expansion. Based on this and international experience, applying the same open access principles to fixed
broadband—through shared ducts, poles, and middle and last mile fiber networks—could boost competition, reduce
costs, and improve service quality across Indonesia.

Fixed broadband
Expanding fixed broadband access is essential for
Indonesia’s shift toward digital productivity. Fixed
broadband—delivered through fiber, fixed wireless
access (FWA), or satellite links—provides the stable,
high-capacity connectivity required for households,
businesses, and public facilities to move from basic
internet access to data-driven production and service
delivery. Fiber networks remain the “gold standard,”
capable of delivering multi-gigabit speeds with low
latency and almost unlimited scalability. FWA and
satellite play a complementary role by extending

coverage to areas where fiber deployment is not
yet viable, though their performance can fluctuate
depending on spectrum availability, signal conditions,
and type and number of concurrent users. Unlike
mobile broadband, which primarily serves users on
the move and shares limited spectrum among many
users, fixed broadband is designed to support highbandwidth, always-on applications with more reliable
throughput. AI and other data-intensive tools amplify
the costs of slow and unreliable broadband, making
fixed connections increasingly essential for firms,
schools, and health facilities. Fiber also underpins the
wider connectivity ecosystem: fiberized towers and
backhaul sustain 5G expansion, while also relieving
pressure on mobile networks.

Coverage has expanded recently, but uptake
remains shallow and uneven. 39 million homes—
nearly half of all households—are within reach of a
fixed broadband network, yet only 17.4 million (22
percent) are connected.93 This leaves a “coverage gap”
of 45 million homes and a “usage gap” of 21.6 million.94
Nearly all households are electrified, suggesting that the
challenge lies not in general infrastructure availability
but in affordability and demand. Uptake is highest in
Java-Bali (28 percent) and falls to just 4 and 3 percent
in Papua and Maluku, respectively.95 Average fixed
broadband speeds (41 Mbps) are third last in ASEAN,
ahead only of Myanmar and Timor-Leste. This is not due
to infrastructure or technical constraints, but, instead,
a commercial decision by ISPs: most offer entry-level
packages at 30-50 Mbps96 to segment customers and
maintain affordability, whereas in neighboring markets,
and under Komdigi’s own target, the baseline is now
100 Mbps. For Telkomsel’s Indihome, the overwhelming
market leader, average household revenue per user
(ARPU) declined to about IDR 217,000 (US$13-14)
per month in 2025, down from around IDR 250,000
two years earlier, reflecting growing competition that
continues to drive prices downward, but not yet at a
level that makes fixed broadband widely affordable.97
As noted earlier, the cost of fixed broadband internet
in Malaysia, Thailand, and Viet Nam is half or less of
Indonesia’s in GNI per capita terms.

remains insufficient to counter strong market
concentration. Telkom Group has roughly two-thirds
of all retail fixed broadband subscriptions, followed

and access network, and to a lesser extent PLN’s fiber
network, continue to determine the market structure.

Competition remains strongest in urban areas, while
smaller ISPs in secondary cities and rural districts
face high entry barriers due to limited availability of
passive infrastructure and continued dependence
on incumbent wholesale networks, which drives up
backbone access costs. A more competitive wholesale
environment would expand the overall market, as lower
prices and better service quality can unlock substantial
new demand—benefiting all ISPs.


FWA is emerging as an important complement
to fiber in expanding broadband coverage. The
2025 auction of 1.4 GHz spectrum to two ISPs marks
an important step, enabling delivery of last mile
connectivity and more affordable plans in rural and
peri-urban areas. Surge, one of the ISPs, has announced
plans for 100 Mbps at IDR 100,000 per month.99
However, the 1.4 GHz band is not commonly used
for FWA globally, and compatible customer premises
equipment (CPE) remains limited and thus costly.100
FWA can quickly bring homes and small businesses
online where fiber is not yet viable, but cannot match
the speed and reliability of FTTP, as performance
depends on spectrum availability, signal quality,
and line-of-sight. As additional and more standard
spectrum bands are refarmed and MNOs deploy
5G-based FWA, performance and cost efficiency are
expected to improve substantially. Even so, FWA should
be seen as a complementary solution: it can extend
connectivity quickly where fiber is not yet viable, while
fiber will remain essential for higher speeds, capacity,
and long-term network scalability. In the most remote
and commercially challenging areas, however, FWA
may still offer a practical enduring option.

High-speed internet at schools, health centers,
and local governments is critical for Indonesia’s
digital transformation and improvements in service
delivery, yet current levels are alarmingly low. As

These public facilities are also the foundation
for community-level digital infrastructure. Rural

centers, or local training hubs, which limits the ability of
citizens and MSMEs in these areas to meaningfully use

sharing, and weak wholesale competition continue
to constrain middle and last mile network
expansion and affordability. Civil works are expensive
and time-consuming due to inconsistent and often

local governments. The 2020 Omnibus Law on
Job Creation formally opened utility and transport
infrastructure for fair shared use, lowering barriers for

ensure neutral access have led to strong results for
competition and retail affordability.111 Likewise, if PLN
were to open access to its poles, it could both monetize
a valuable national asset and reduce rural broadband
deployment costs by as much as two-thirds. For
example, Nepal has achieved roughly twice Indonesia’s

Improving in-building connectivity is also
important for realizing the benefits of network
investment. Many households cannot fully benefit
from high-speed access because of outdated modems
or legacy Wi-Fi equipment that support only older
standards (typically Wi-Fi 4). As a result, effective
speeds often fall short of network capacity. Komdigi’s
2025 support of Wi-Fi 6E/7 making the lower 6 GHz
band available for Wi-Fi provides an opportunity to
raise performance standards and encourage device
upgrades.112 Promoting adoption of newer Wi-Fi 5 and
6 standards through updated technical guidelines and
consumer awareness campaigns could enhance enduser speeds. Requiring fiber-ready in-building wiring in
new housing and public facilities would further ensure
that high-capacity networks deliver their intended
performance.

Satellites provide a complementary pathway for
remote and underserved regions. The government’s
SATRIA-1 geostationary earth orbit (GEO) satellite,113
launched through a PPP in 2023 following a 2019
tender, has a total capacity of 150 Gbps and originally
aimed to connect 150,000 public service points in
unserved and underserved areas across Indonesia.114
After launch, the target was reduced to 50,000 and then
37,000—and installation has advanced well against this
adjusted scope, with 27,805 locations connected by
mid-2025.115 However, around three-fifths of activated
sites are in Sumatra, Java, and Sulawesi—areas where
terrestrial fiber and FWA deployment are feasible and
more optimal—while less than 12 percent are in Papua
and Maluku, where sparse populations and challenging
terrain make satellite the most effective solution. With
SATRIA-1’s capacity finite,116 refocusing its use toward
public facilities in the most remote areas, particularly
in eastern Indonesia, that are unlikely to be feasibly
served by terrestrial technologies in the near-term
would help ensure the satellite delivers the greatest
impact and most efficiently. This need is significant: in
Papua and Maluku alone, an estimated 4,861 schools,
380 health clinics, and 8,209 village offices still lack any
internet connection (see Figure B.13), with many more
relying on limited mobile coverage.


The 2024 launch of Starlink’s low earth orbit (LEO)
satellites117 has been positively disruptive for the
market in Indonesia. Demand has surged—not only
in rural areas lacking reliable terrestrial networks, but
also in cities like Jakarta, where Starlink was temporarily
oversubscribed until additional capacity was added in
mid-2025.118 This strong uptake, despite prices of IDR
400,000-700,000 per month, highlights the appetite
among households and firms for faster and more
reliable internet. Starlink and similar LEO systems
are also increasingly being used to provide satellite
backhaul for mobile towers in remote regions, helping
extend terrestrial coverage and provide a reliable
backup option. Over the longer term, LEO systems
are best positioned to serve rural communities and
maritime regions, while GEO and medium earth
orbit (MEO) satellites will continue to play important
roles in providing backhaul, broadcast services, and
resilient connectivity for remote public facilities.

Satellite capacity is also essential for emergency
communications and redundancy in a country highly
exposed to natural disasters. Emerging direct-to-device
(D2D) technologies119 could further extend connectivity
by allowing standard smartphones to connect directly
to satellites, improving coverage in remote areas, at
sea, and during emergencies.

Data centers and cloud
Data centers are critical enablers of productivity in
a digital economy, allowing firms and governments
to store, process, and analyze data efficiently,
supporting automation, logistics optimization,
digital finance, and e-commerce—all of which can
raise productivity and create higher-skilled jobs.

Data centers also facilitate technology transfer from
foreign investment: global operators bring expertise
in energy management, cybersecurity, and operations
that can strengthen Indonesia’s domestic capabilities.

As demand for computing power grows—driven by
cloud services, data-intensive applications, and AI—
Indonesia will need to expand domestic data center
capacity to meet growing local needs. At the same
time, Indonesia has a strategic opportunity to attract
regional workloads, bringing investment, better jobs,
and deeper integration into regional digital value
chains. Achieving this requires strong integration
with regional and global networks because cloud and
platform companies place regional capacity where
latency is lowest and international routes are most
diverse. High-quality interconnection is therefore
essential for Indonesia to compete as a regional hub
and to support local innovation.

Indonesia’s data center market is growing but
capacity remains modest and low in per capita
terms compared to ASEAN peers. Total operational
capacity in Indonesia reached around 333 MW in mid2025 (1.2 watts per capita) and is projected to rise to
456 MW by end-2025, well behind Malaysia, which is
expected to rise to 1,216 MW in the same period (22
watts per capita) (Figure B.14).121 In 2025, Thailand’s
pipeline capacity grew faster than Indonesia. Industry
forecasts have Indonesia reaching the RPJMN 2029
target of 2,000 MW (6.9 watts per capita) a year late,
in 2030. Over half of existing capacity (57 percent)
is in Jabodetabek,122 followed by Batam (7 percent),
Surabaya (6 percent), and other cities across Java,
Sumatra, and Bali (30 percent) (Figure B.15). This
concentration reflects proximity to customers and
backbone connectivity, but it also creates dependence,
puts pressure on local electricity networks, and limits
resilience.


Reliable, scalable, and increasingly renewable
electricity has become a decisive factor in global
data center location decisions, especially for AIready data centers. AI workloads are far more
energy-intensive than traditional applications, sharply
increasing demand for reliable, high-capacity and
renewable power in data center hubs. In Indonesia,
long lead times for electricity connections—often up
to three years in Jabodetabek—combined with limited
access to renewable energy constrain the expansion
of existing hubs and weaken competitiveness relative
to Malaysia and Thailand, which offer faster grid
connections and clearer renewable energy pathways.

Batam has strong potential to evolve into a renewable
energy–powered data center hub, supported by
emerging regional collaboration initiatives with
Singapore and Malaysia under the framework of the
ASEAN Power Grid. Over time, and as demand grows
and grid readiness improves, locations with substantial
geothermal, hydro, or solar resources—such as North
Sumatra and parts of Sulawesi—could emerge as
future secondary hubs. Leveraging these resources,
alongside grid upgrades and improved planning,
will be essential to attract hyperscale investment and
position Indonesia as a competitive regional hub.

Growth is further constrained by uncertainty
regarding data regulations, complex permitting,
and fragmented investment facilitation. Overlapping
general and sectoral regulations on data governance
and cross-border flows, compounded by limited
implementing guidelines and uncertainty about how
rules will be enforced or evolved, raise compliance
costs and discourage investment. Some data center
operators have reported needing more than 100
permissions.123 Although some permits have been
streamlined through the Online Single Submission
(OSS) Risk-Based Approach, others that are mediumand high-risk have processes outside of the OSS,
leading to cumulative timeline of 12-24 months,
compared with 3-9 months in Malaysia and Thailand.

Combined with the current exclusion of Jakarta as a
designated international submarine cable landing site
and restrictions on foreign-flagged cable-repair vessels,
these challenges constrain investor confidence and
delay project delivery. As discussed in the ‘Backbone’
section, international connectivity and domestic IXPs
are essential for data centers to deliver low-latency
performance and for Indonesia’s firms to participate
more in the global digital economy.

Device affordability
High smartphone adoption reflects improving
device affordability, with opportunities to make
it more inclusive. 74 percent of adults own a

affordable in urban areas with higher incomes but
remain costly in poorer, rural regions. The growing
secondhand market is helping narrow this gap, with

and outpacing new phones (1 percent) through

### 4. Conclusion and Recommendations
Digital transformation can make an important
contribution to Indonesia’s future growth but
unlocking its economic potential will require a new
focus toward higher-quality digital infrastructure.

Indonesia has expanded access rapidly, but the
next phase will require closing persistent coverage
gaps while simultaneously improving service quality,
affordability and data center capacity so that digital
tools can be used productively nationwide. Predictable
regulation, stronger competition, wider infrastructure
sharing, and a more proactive government role—
including leveraging public sector resources and
demand—will all be essential to mobilize the much
greater private investment needed to deliver. This
also means treating digital infrastructure as a strategic
enabling sector, whose growth is critical for the wider
economy—and not primarily as a revenue source.

Without timely reforms, Indonesia risks losing ground
to regional competitors racing to capture digital
infrastructure investments, especially to prepare for
the AI-driven economy.

The following four objectives and seven
recommendations set out the highest-impact
near-term reforms to simultaneously strengthen
Indonesia’s digital infrastructure and close the
remaining gaps in access. Together, they address
the core bottlenecks that limit the ability of digital

infrastructure to drive productivity, growth, and job
creation in Indonesia. While not exhaustive, these
priorities focus on the reforms with the greatest
potential to deliver near-term impact and crowd in
greater private investment. Looking ahead, it will
also be important to develop a more comprehensive
national digital infrastructure plan to guide long-term
investment and coordination.

At the same time, the impact of better digital
infrastructure will be greatest when accompanied
by complementary reforms and investments across
the digital ecosystem. Prioritization should be given
to advancing digital skills, improving governance
and regulatory predictability, accelerating the digital
transformation of government, safeguarding digital
trust through stronger personal data protection and
cybersecurity frameworks, and scaling DPI. Together,
these measures will help ensure that improved
connectivity and expanded data center capacity
translate into wider access to economic opportunities,
stronger job creation, higher productivity, and faster
growth of Indonesia’s digital economy.
